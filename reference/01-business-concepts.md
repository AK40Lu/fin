# Business Concepts — Taught Through the Idea

> Fin loads this when introducing or explaining a business concept.
> Every concept here includes: what it is, how to introduce it without jargon, and how to connect it to the kid's actual idea.

---

## Rule Before Using This File

Never teach a concept in the abstract. Before each entry below is a "connect to idea" prompt. Use the kid's actual business (bakery, dog-walking, etc.) as the example — always.

---

## The Core Concepts (in teaching order)

### 1. Customer
**What it is:** The specific person who will pay money for what you're selling. Not "everyone." A real human with a reason to buy.

**Introduce by asking:** "Who would actually buy this — not who you wish would buy it. One real person, with a name and a reason."

**Common mistake to fix:** "Everyone wants this." Fin's response: "Everyone is not a customer. Let's start with one."

**Connect to idea:** "For your cookie business — who is your first customer? Not your parents — they'll buy because they love you. Someone who would buy because they *want* the cookie."

---

### 2. Value Proposition
**What it is:** The reason a customer chooses your product over every other option — including doing nothing.

**Introduce by asking:** "Why would someone buy from you instead of just going to the store? What's the real reason?"

**Common mistake to fix:** "Because mine are better." Fin's response: "Better how? Better enough to go out of their way and pay more? Let's get specific."

**Connect to idea:** "For a dog-walking business — the Rover app exists. What does buying from you, a neighbor they know, give them that an app doesn't?"

---

### 3. Cost
**What it is:** Everything you spend to make one unit of your product or deliver one unit of your service.

**Introduce by asking:** "Before you tell me how much you want to charge — what does it cost to make one? Let's add it up together."

**Components to walk through:**
- Materials / ingredients
- Packaging (bags, boxes, labels)
- Time (if the kid wants to include it — frame this honestly: your time has value)
- Any tools or equipment amortized across sales

**Connect to idea:** "For cookies: flour, sugar, butter, eggs, chocolate chips, the bag they come in. Write down each one, how much you use per dozen, and what that piece costs."

---

### 4. Price
**What it is:** The amount the customer pays. Not what you want to make — what the customer will actually pay.

**Introduce by asking:** "What would you charge? Now tell me — what made you pick that number?"

**Two price traps to catch:**
1. Price below cost → "You're paying customers to take your product. Let's fix that."
2. Price based on what the kid wants to earn, not what customers will pay → "What would your target customer actually pay? How do we find out?"

**Strategies to introduce (one at a time):**
- Cost-plus: cost + desired profit = price
- Competitive: look at what others charge, price relative to it
- Value-based: charge based on what the customer gets, not what it costs you

---

### 5. Profit
**What it is:** What's left after subtracting cost from price. The actual money you make.

**Introduce by asking:** "You sell it for $X and it costs you $Y to make. What's left?"

**Formula:** Price – Cost = Profit per unit

**Connect to life:** "If you sell 10 dozen cookies this week at $5.30 profit each, that's $53. That's yours — not before expenses, not 'revenue' — that's money in your pocket."

**Profit margin:** Profit ÷ Price × 100 = Margin %. Introduce only after profit is solid.

---

### 6. Revenue vs. Profit
**What it is:** Revenue is total sales. Profit is what's left after costs.

**Introduce when:** The kid talks about "making $50" when they mean they sold $50 worth — and haven't subtracted their costs yet.

**Introduce by asking:** "You sold $50 worth of cookies. How much did it cost you to make them? The difference — that's what you actually made."

**Analogy:** "Imagine you mow lawns for $20, but the gas costs $5. You didn't make $20. You made $15."

---

### 7. Customer Discovery
**What it is:** Finding out what real customers actually want — before you build or invest too much.

**Introduce when:** The kid has an idea but hasn't talked to a single potential customer.

**Introduce by asking:** "Before we figure out how to make more of them — have you asked anyone who isn't your family if they'd actually buy one?"

**The test:** "Find one person outside your family. Tell them what you're selling and at what price. Listen to what they say. Don't explain why it's worth it — just listen."

**Why it matters:** "The fastest way to find out if an idea works is to try to sell it to one real person. Not to think about selling it. To actually try."

---

### 8. Marketing
**What it is:** How you tell potential customers that your product exists.

**Introduce when:** The kid has a product and a price but no customers beyond people who already know about them.

**Introduce by asking:** "How would someone who doesn't know you find out that you sell [cookies / keychains / etc.]?"

**Low-cost channels for kids:**
- Word of mouth (the most powerful — and hardest to control)
- Flyers in the neighborhood
- Telling one trusted adult who knows many people
- A simple Instagram or Facebook post (if age-appropriate — check with parent)

**The lesson:** "Marketing isn't about being everywhere. It's about being where your customers are, saying one clear thing."

---

### 9. The Pitch
**What it is:** A short, clear explanation of what you're selling, why someone should want it, and what it costs.

**Introduce when:** The kid is about to interact with a potential customer for the first time.

**Introduce by asking:** "If someone you didn't know walked by your table, what would you say to get them to stop?"

**The formula (simplified):**
1. What it is (one sentence)
2. Why they'd want it (one sentence)
3. What it costs

**Practice:** "Say it to me right now like I'm a stranger walking past. Go."

---

### 10. Scale
**What it is:** What happens when you try to sell 10x more than you do now. Can you do it? What breaks?

**Introduce when:** The kid talks about "becoming a big business" or asks how to make a lot more money.

**Introduce by asking:** "Right now you make 2 dozen cookies a week. If you wanted to make 20 dozen — what would need to change?"

**Things that often break at scale:**
- Time (can't do it all alone)
- Ingredients (have to buy in bulk, manage inventory)
- Customers (harder to find, need marketing)
- Quality (harder to control)

**The lesson:** "Scaling isn't just doing more. It's redesigning how you do it. Some businesses can scale. Some are intentionally small. Both are okay — as long as you're choosing."
