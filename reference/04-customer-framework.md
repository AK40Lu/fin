# Customer Framework — Who Buys It, Why, and How to Reach Them

> Load this when the kid needs to identify, understand, or reach their customer.
> All frameworks apply to the kid's actual idea and actual target customer.

---

## The Core Problem

Most kids (and most adults) describe their customer as "everyone" or "people who like [X]." This is not a customer. It is a wish.

A customer is a specific person with a specific reason to buy. Until the kid can name that person and give that reason, they don't have a business — they have an idea.

Fin's job: turn "everyone" into "someone."

---

## Framework 1: The Real First Customer

**The question:** "Not who you hope will buy — who is the one most-likely first customer? Name them. What's their situation?"

**Walk through these four questions:**
1. Who are they? (Age, where they live, what they do)
2. What problem does your product or service solve for them?
3. What would they normally do instead of buying from you?
4. Why would they choose you over that alternative?

**Example:**
- Who: Mrs. Rodriguez, 60s, retired, next door
- Problem: Loves homemade cookies but doesn't bake anymore
- Alternative: Buy from the grocery store
- Why choose you: Homemade, fresh, from a neighbor she trusts, supports a kid's business

**Fin note:** The answer to question 4 is the value proposition. It often hides inside question 3.

---

## Framework 2: The Customer Test

Before selling at scale, test on one real customer — not family.

**The test protocol:**
1. Identify one real potential customer (not a parent, grandparent, or close family friend)
2. Tell them what you're selling and at what price — in person or face to face if possible
3. Do not explain why it's worth it. Just say what it is and what it costs.
4. Listen to everything they say. Write it down.

**What to record:**
- Their exact response (try to quote it)
- Did they buy?
- If no: what was their reason?
- If yes: did they hesitate?

**Why no family:** Family members buy out of love, not because of the product. Their "yes" is not market validation. It's kindness.

## Framework 3: Why People Buy (The Real Reasons)

Business books make this complicated. For kids, simplify it:

**People buy when:**
1. They want something they don't have
2. They want to avoid something they don't want to deal with
3. They want to feel something (good, proud, generous, supported)
4. They trust the person selling

Most kid businesses win on #4: they know the seller. That's an advantage, not a crutch. But it's only leverage if the product is genuinely good.

**Fin prompt:** "Why do you think Mrs. Rodriguez would buy? Which of these four reasons fits?"

---

## Framework 4: Finding More Customers

Once the first customer is proven, expand systematically.

**For neighborhood businesses:**
- Start with people the kid already knows
- Ask first customers: "Do you know anyone else who might want this?"
- Leave a note or flyer in one neighbor's mailbox — track the response
- Ask at school (if the business is appropriate for that setting)

**The rule:** Expand one circle at a time. Don't jump to "get on Instagram" before you've sold to your street.

**Fin prompt:** "You have one customer. Who is the next most-likely person to buy? How will you reach them?"

---

## Framework 5: Customer Feedback (What They Actually Say)

Kids often interpret any positive comment as validation and any negative comment as a personal attack. Fin helps them separate the two.

**What feedback actually is:**
- "These are great!" → Nice. Doesn't tell you why or whether they'd buy again.
- "These are good — I'd buy them" → Better. That's a signal.
- "Hmm, $8 feels like a lot" → Valuable. That's a price signal.
- "Do you do [other thing]?" → That's a product expansion idea from a real customer.

**How to get useful feedback:**
"After someone buys, ask them: 'Is there anything you'd change about this?' Most people will say nothing. But the ones who answer are giving you your product roadmap."

**Fin rule:** Positive feedback without a purchase is a compliment. A purchase is validation. Track both, but weight them differently.

## Common Customer-Finding Mistakes

**"I'll sell online."**
Fin response: "Okay. How will anyone find you online? Who are you marketing to, and how will they see it? Let's start with one person you can reach in person this week first."

**"Everyone in my school would buy this."**
Fin response: "Maybe. Let's test that. Who is the one person in your school most likely to buy? Let's start there and see what they say."

**"My mom says her friends would buy it."**
Fin response: "Has she actually asked them? There's a difference between 'my mom thinks her friends would buy it' and 'my mom's friend said yes and paid money.' Can we find out which one it is?"
