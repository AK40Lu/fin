# Parent Co-Learner Guide

> Fin loads this when parent dynamics need navigating — parent taking over, parent absent, parent asking questions about their role.

---

## What "Co-Learner" Actually Means

You are not the supervisor. You are not the investor. You are not the teacher.

You are someone who is learning alongside your kid — in real time — how a business gets built. Most parents know far more about business than their 11-year-old does. That is exactly why this role is hard. The knowledge you have will constantly tempt you to step in, explain, and solve.

The rule: you can contribute, but the kid decides.

If your kid makes a pricing decision you think is wrong, your job is to ask a question, not correct. "What made you pick that number?" is a co-learner question. "That's too low — you should charge more" is a parent-as-manager move, and it costs your kid the learning.

---

## The Three Things Co-Learners Do

**1. Ask questions, don't give answers.**

Instead of: "You should sell at the farmer's market."
Try: "What places do you think your customers hang out?"

Instead of: "Your price is too low."
Try: "Have you figured out what it costs you to make one yet?"

Instead of: "You need a flyer."
Try: "How are people going to find out about this?"

**2. Enable, don't execute.**

There are things only you can do: drive to the store, help set up a table, buy supplies. Do those things when asked. But write the list, plan the route, and make the pitch — that belongs to your kid.

If your kid asks you to write the flyer, say: "Tell me what you want it to say and I'll type it up." That's enabling. Writing it for them is executing.

**3. Show up honestly.**

Your kid is learning what it looks like to try something, struggle with it, and figure it out. That's more valuable than having a successful bakery at age 11.

If you bail on the role — skip sessions, take over, lose interest — they'll notice. What you model matters more than what Fin says.

---

## When to Step In (and When Not To)

**Step in:**
- Safety or ethics (if your kid wants to do something dishonest or unsafe)
- Logistics they genuinely cannot handle without an adult
- When they ask — and only when they ask

**Do not step in:**
- When they make a business decision you disagree with (let them learn from it)
- When they're struggling to figure something out (the struggle is the lesson)
- When they're frustrated (see the next section)
- When they want to quit (let Fin handle this — your job is presence, not problem-solving)

---

## When Your Kid Says "I Want to Quit"

Don't panic. Don't lecture. Don't motivate.

Say: "Okay. Tell me what's going on."

Then listen. After they've talked, ask: "What would it take to want to keep going?"

You're not convincing them. You're helping them hear themselves. Let Fin carry the coaching weight on this. Your job is to be someone safe to say hard things to.

## What Fin Will Ask of You

At the end of every session, Fin will give you a brief co-learner summary — usually two or three things:

1. **What concept your kid worked on** — so you can reference it during the week
2. **One specific thing to help with** — concrete, small, enabling (not solving)
3. **One specific thing not to do** — the place where parents most often accidentally block learning

Read these. Take them seriously. They're the coaching equivalent of a training plan — tailored to what your kid specifically needs right now.

---

## Hard Truths for Parents

**You might know more. That's not the point.**

The point is for your kid to figure things out. A business coach who builds the plan isn't teaching. They're consulting. Fin is coaching — and so are you.

**Your anxiety is not a signal that something is going wrong.**

Your kid pricing something too high, making a bad batch of cookies, or not getting their first sale on the first try — these are not failures. These are sessions. Treat them as such.

**If you can't be a co-learner, say so.**

Some parents discover they can't resist taking over. That's okay — but name it. Fin can adjust the dynamic. The worst outcome is a parent who's nominally present but actually running things, while the kid learns to defer rather than decide.

---

## What You're Actually Teaching

You are teaching your kid what it looks like when an adult takes something seriously, stays curious, admits what they don't know, and keeps going when it doesn't work.

The business is a vehicle. The lesson is bigger than the business.
